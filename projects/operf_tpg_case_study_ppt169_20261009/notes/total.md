The two parties are a pension investor and a private-equity manager. OPERF is Oregon's public pension investor and a limited partner in this case. With that map, the letter's effects are clear. The optional 10% reduction would take OPERF's commitment from $750 million to $675 million. # 08_part2_question

Part 2 asks why TPG is revising its terms amid the 2008 crisis. This is the strategic question, Question 1, and Li continues. We will look at the crisis, at one specific investment, at the investors' own pressures, and then at what each concession does for TPG.

---

# 09_crisis_channels

Start with the external shock to this investment structure. The crisis hit through two channels. In the financing channel, the credit contraction made it harder to finance acquisitions and refinance debt, which limits what a fund can buy and support. In the exit channel, falling public-market valuations and an uncertain outlook weakened sale valuations, IPO prospects and exit timing, so getting capital back became slower and less certain. These are industry-level shocks. The transmission mechanism here is the team's analysis, read with the Federal Reserve's statement of 16 December 2008 on credit conditions and the weaker outlook. For TPG, one specific investment made the question of risk judgment much more direct, and that is next.

---

# 10_wamu_timeline

Now return to the WaMu stake that appeared in the structure diagram. On 11 April 2008, Washington Mutual, Inc. disclosed an investment agreement that named TPG VI. On 25 September 2008, Washington Mutual Bank was closed. Note that the holding company and the bank are different legal entities, and the timeline is a sequence, not to scale. By December, limited partners had reason to scrutinise TPG's investment selection and risk assessment. That may add to TPG's pressure to maintain investor confidence, but one investment does not represent the performance of the whole fund. Beyond how they view the manager, LPs faced a more practical problem: when distributions are uncertain, what happens to their existing commitments and their asset allocation?

---

# 11_liquidity_allocation

Two separate pressures. On the liquidity side, less predictable distributions meet binding commitments, which can create funding strain for some LPs. OPERF's own team, however, believed it could meet its calls, so the case for reducing cannot simply be that OPERF lacks cash. On the allocation side, the PE weight is PE value divided by total portfolio value. When public assets fall faster than private-equity marks adjust, the PE weight can rise passively, and that calls for a separate allocation judgment. We assume no fixed PE value here; it is a qualitative mechanism. The closing line is the one to keep: payment capacity and portfolio allocation are separate decisions. These different constraints shape how willing LPs are to keep supporting the fund, so now we turn the perspective back to TPG.

---

# 12_lp_support_branches

From TPG's side, LP support matters in two places. For the current fund, reliable capital calls support investment execution. For future funds, LP willingness to reinvest affects access to new capital, and that is a possible future effect rather than a confirmed one. The case gives us Schmitz's view of the fundraising environment, not a statement of TPG's intentions, so protecting future capital is a plausible motive, not a proven one. This explains why a manager would feel pressure to respond. The next slide compares what each concession resolves and what it costs TPG.

---

# 13_concession_matrix

Each concession has an economic cost, but each can also make funding more predictable and strengthen the relationship. The commitment option can ease funding strain and reduce the risk of LPs failing to meet calls, at the cost of less deployable capital if LPs exercise it. The fee cut shares the burden and supports LP confidence, at the cost of lower fee revenue. The call-approval threshold improves cash-planning predictability, at the cost of an extra approval step above the threshold. Our conclusion for Question 1 is that TPG could accept limited economic concessions to preserve reliable LP support while keeping the fund able to invest. This is a possible explanation; it does not prove that defaults were avoided or that confidence was restored. Question 2 now asks what management fees and carry each do, and what incentive problems remain after these concessions.

# 14_part3_question

Part 3 asks whether management fees and carry align GP and LP interests. This is the incentive question, Question 2, and Li presents it. We look at what each form of compensation is for, how the fee base creates its own incentive, and how the GP's own capital changes the picture.

---

# 15_fees_vs_carry

We have explained why TPG was willing to cut its fee; now look at the compensation mechanism being adjusted. Management fees support the team and its operating costs; they are charged periodically on the fee base agreed in the partnership agreement. Carried interest rewards distributable investment profits, determined under the distribution waterfall, and it is paid only when the agreed conditions are met. A salary-and-bonus analogy helps, with two cautions: the fee is not a personal salary, and carry is not an annual charge on fund size. In a whole-fund structure the steps are to return contributed capital, apply the agreed waterfall terms, and then allocate the remaining profits. Any preferred return, catch-up and other provisions depend on the LPA, and this illustration follows Metrick and Yasuda's description rather than TPG VI's actual contract. Because the two forms of pay rest on different bases, what the fee cut changes about incentives depends on the scale of fees, which is next.

---

# 16_fee_base_incentive

Put the letter back into this fee mechanism. The bars are illustrative annual management-fee revenue on the $19.8 billion fund: $297 million at the original 1.50%, $267.3 million after the cut to 1.35%, and $240.57 million if the fee base were also 10% smaller, which is a hypothetical. The fee cut works through the rate and the commitment reduction works through the base, and together they lower the fee burden. But neither turns the management fee into pay that depends on realised profits, so a size incentive can remain. These are uniform annual figures before offsets and costs, not actual receipts. Still, it would be wrong to describe the GP as collecting fees without bearing investment risk, and for that we go back to the manager's own capital in the structure diagram.

---

# 17_gp_capital_exposure

In the structure diagram the GP is also an investor, so if investments fail, its own capital can be lost. The matrix sets carry beside GP invested capital. Carry offers potential performance compensation when there are qualifying profits and may simply be absent when there are losses. GP capital earns an investment return under the agreed terms when things go well and can be lost when they do not. The case notes TPG's roughly $500 million own commitment. So the manager has a profit motive and also a real downside exposure. That strengthens alignment, but it does not remove every conflict, and the actual sharing of losses depends on fund terms including any clawback. Question 2 has described the mechanisms. Question 3 translates them into cash flows and quantifies what each side actually gets, and Alex takes it from here.

# 40_hurdle_vs_carry

The two changes affect investors in different ways. A hurdle determines what investors receive before TPG earns carry: investors get the accrued preferred return first, although the hurdle does not guarantee that return. Catch-up determines how much of that advantage persists; with full catch-up TPG can recover its agreed profit share after investors receive the preference, and without catch-up investors keep more of the benefit. Lower carry changes the share of eligible profit TPG receives, but on its own it does not create a preferred return. Contemporary evidence gives a market reference: in Metrick and Yasuda's July 2007 sample, 93.1% of 144 buyout funds used hurdles and 8% was the most common rate, while the same terms table recorded no buyout funds with carry below 20%. That is a historical sample, not the whole market, and TPG VI's exact hurdle and catch-up terms remain unconfirmed. In this discussion LP means the investors and GP means TPG, and the model returns contributed capital, including contributions used for fees, before paying the preferred return and carry. First, how does value change as we vary the hurdle while keeping carry at 20%?

---
